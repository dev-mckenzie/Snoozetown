## The problem

Parents and caregivers of disabled people in Springfield, Missouri, and the Southwest Missouri
region must deal with many programs. Each program has its own rules, prerequisites, documents,
wait lists, and offices. Caregivers are already at their limit. Our job: map the system
exactly, find where families get stuck, and collect the facts we need to build three products.

## Who we serve

- Ages: transition age (16–25) and adults (21 and older).
- Disability types: intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD), physical and medically
  complex conditions, and sensory disabilities (Deaf, hard of hearing, blind, low vision).
  Include mental health only where it co-occurs with these or acts as a gate to a service.
- Area: the Southwest Missouri region, centered on Springfield (Greene County). Use the service
  area of the state offices that serve Springfield (for example, the DMH Division of
  Developmental Disabilities regional office that serves Greene County). Name the counties you
  include.
- The client: a professional who helps families (for example, a case manager or advocate).

## Pain points the client named

1. DD eligibility and Medicaid waivers (DMH DD eligibility, wait lists, waiver rules).
2. Income and benefit rules (SSI, SSDI, Childhood Disability Benefits, MO HealthNet, spend-down,
   asset limits, ABLE accounts).
3. School-to-adult transition (leaving school, guardianship versus supported decision-making,
   adult case management).
4. Daily life services (housing, transport, work and Vocational Rehabilitation, respite, home
   care, equipment).

## Final products (the lead builds these later from your research)

1. Family navigator guide in two levels: plain language for caregivers, and a detailed version
   for professionals.
2. Eligibility screener: a web page. A caregiver answers questions. The page lists the programs
   the person may qualify for and the next steps. It runs in the browser and saves no data.
3. Gap and policy report for: state lawmakers and agencies; local government and county SB40
   boards; providers and nonprofits.

## Rules for every agent

1. Research only. Do not contact any person or agency. Do not send email, post, submit forms,
   create accounts, or sign up for anything. Do not edit, commit, or push to any git
   repository. Write only to your own file in the team folder.
2. Accuracy is a safety matter. Families will act on this work. Never invent a phone number,
   address, dollar amount, age limit, deadline, or URL. Every fact needs a source URL. Prefer
   primary sources: .gov sites, statutes, regulations, agency manuals, official program pages.
   Use secondary sources only to find primary ones, or label them as secondary.
3. Give the year of each number (for example, "2026 SSI federal benefit rate"). If 2027 numbers
   are already published, give both.
4. If you cannot verify a fact, put it in "Open questions". Do not guess.
5. Mark each card with a confidence level: High (primary source checked today), Medium
   (secondary source, or a primary source that may be old), Low (could not confirm).
6. Writing style: never use an em dash or an en dash as punctuation. A dash inside a number or
   date range (for example 16–25) is fine. Use short sentences, active voice, and simple words
   (ASD-STE100 style).
7. Stay inside your role. If a topic belongs to another role, note it in one line and leave the
   detail to them. The roles are: 01 advocate, 02 social scientist, 03 physician, 04 legal,
   05 political action, 06 benefits planner, 07 transition and employment, 08 community living
   and home care.
8. Write your full findings to the file path in your prompt. Write the file in parts as you go,
   so that work is not lost. Your final reply to the lead must be short: the file path, the 5
   most important findings, and the top open questions. Maximum 300 words.
