# Gaps in Disability Services in Southwest Missouri

**A gap and policy report on services for disabled young adults and adults (age 16 and older) and their caregivers**

Date: 27 October 2026

Prepared for: state lawmakers and state agencies; local governments and county SB40 developmental disability boards; providers and nonprofits.

## 1. Purpose

This report shows where the public service system fails disabled people age 16 and older, and their caregivers, in Southwest Missouri. It covers intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD), physical and medically complex conditions, and sensory disabilities. For each gap it gives the effect on families, the evidence, the cause (law, rule, budget, or practice), and who can fix it. It ends with dated recommendations. Facts come from public sources checked on 26–27 October 2026. Facts our fact checks could not confirm are marked "not verified."

The region is the 23 counties of the Department of Mental Health (DMH) Division of Developmental Disabilities (DDD) Springfield Regional Office (Christian, Dallas, Douglas, Greene, Hickory, Laclede, Ozark, Polk, Stone, Taney, Webster, Wright) and Joplin Satellite Office (Barry, Barton, Cedar, Dade, Henry, Jasper, Lawrence, McDonald, Newton, St. Clair, Vernon) [9].

**Terms.** MO HealthNet: Missouri Medicaid. DSS: Department of Social Services (FSD: its Family Support Division). DHSS: Department of Health and Senior Services. DESE: Department of Elementary and Secondary Education. VR: Missouri Vocational Rehabilitation. Comprehensive, Community Support, Partnership for Hope (PfH): the adult DD waivers. PON: priority of need. ISP: individual support plan. CDS: consumer directed (home care) services. DSP: direct support professional. HCY: Missouri's EPSDT benefit for people under 21. SB40 board: a county DD board funded by a local levy. WIPA: SSA benefits counseling.

## 2. Summary

### 2.1 The problem

A disabled adult in Southwest Missouri must pass through many separate programs, each with its own gate, documents, office, and deadline. On 15 October 2026, the main gate for adults with developmental disabilities, a Medicaid waiver slot, closed to new enrollment with no end date, and the routes around it (job support, respite, home care, housing, transport) are also limited. The families who must manage this are often rural, poor, or led by an aging caregiver, so they have the least time and help to get through it.

### 2.2 Main findings

1. **The DD waiver wait list has no end date, no named crisis exception, and no public count** [1][2]. Budget law limits new slots to 127 named exceptions [13].
2. **Two wait lists meet at school exit.** VR has served only Priority Category I since 9 December 2024 [31][32]. A school leaver with IDD may get a job through VR but have no payer for long-term job support [7][31].
3. **At age 21, MO HealthNet coverage narrows sharply** for therapy, nursing, and devices [36][37][38][39].
4. **The workforce cannot deliver all authorized services.** In 2024, DSP turnover was 46.5%, and 28.0% of agencies turned away referrals for lack of staff [29]. FY2027 rates are flat [13].
5. **Help depends on the county:** SB40 funds, PfH, and local help without Medicaid differ [10][11][22].
6. **Most adults under 63 who are not on a waiver have no paid respite route** [51][55][56], and we found no WIPA project for the region [35].
7. **Families carry the paperwork, and official information is sometimes wrong** [47][64][65][66][67]. Hands-on application help raised enrollment from 6% to 18% in one large trial [71].

### 4.3 Why this creates administrative burden

Administrative burden has three parts [69]. **Learning costs:** six separate front doors (DD eligibility, MO HealthNet, SSI, VR, DHSS home care, county boards), and many families do not know an SSI award does not bring MO HealthNet [45]. **Compliance costs:** records, assessments, renewals, and appeal windows as short as 10 days from mailing [48]; Medicaid forms in a 50-state study read at grade 11 to 18 [80]; nationally, 69% of people who lost Medicaid in the 2023–2024 unwinding lost it for procedural reasons [79]. **Psychological costs:** stress, stigma, and fear of losing benefits by working or saving.

Stress and scarcity reduce the planning and memory that complex processes demand, so burden falls hardest on exhausted caregivers and adults with cognitive disabilities [70]. Distance adds cost: when SSA field offices closed, nearby disability applications fell by about 10%, most among people with low education and earnings [78].

A separate problem looks the same to families: **supply rationing.** Wait lists, closed VR categories, and unstaffed agencies ration services even when a family does every step right. Navigation reduces burden; it cannot create a slot or a worker. The two should be measured separately.

## 5. Findings: the gaps

### Finding 1. The DD waiver wait list has no end date, no named crisis exception, and no public count

**What happens.** A person can be DD eligible and get no waiver services. Since 15 October 2026, new enrollment in the Comprehensive, Community Support, and PfH waivers is wait-listed "due to current budget limitations," with "no estimated end date." Current participants continue. Only Inter-Divisional Agreement cases and cases "mandated by federal rule" keep access; the MOCDD children's waiver continues as usual [1].

**Evidence.**
- The memo names no crisis or emergency exception [1]. The rule gives emergency cases (PON 5) top priority on the list, a place in line, not a slot [3]. Whether an emergency case can enroll now is not verified.
- HB 2010 Section 10.2015: "No funds shall be expended in furtherance of waiver slots assigned on July 1, 2026 except for" 50 crisis residential, 27 nursing home transition, 39 Children's Division transition, and 11 MOCDD transition slots (127) [13].
- Section 10.3005 requires a monthly report to the House Budget and Senate Appropriations chairs and ranking members on "individuals presenting for service that have not been assigned a waiver slot" and the cost to serve them [13]. The DMH wait list report section shows no numbers [2].
- No rule sets a time limit on the wait. Loss of a caregiver counts as an emergency when it happens, not when it is predictable, so the list rewards late contact [3].
- In FY2025, 41.0% of the statewide DD caseload had support coordination or information support only; in the region, 30.4% [8].
- A previous wait list began 1 July 2024 and was cleared after a supplemental signed 17 March 2025 (history from news only) [15][30].
- For FY2027, the legislature reversed proposed cuts to DD self-directed supports and day habilitation; news reports put the total at about $80.7 million (all funds), with no primary source [16][17]. The state projects a FY2028 shortfall of more than $500 million [14].

**Cause.** Budget law (HB 2010 Sections 10.2015 and 10.410); DMH practice under 9 CSR 45-2.015 and 2.017.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly (House Budget and its Health, Mental Health and Social Services subcommittee; Senate Appropriations); the Governor; DMH.

### Finding 2. At school exit, the VR closure meets the waiver wait list

**What happens.** A student with IDD leaving school in 2026–2027 can face two wait lists. In VR Category II or III, the student waits for VR. In Category I, VR can fund job placement and short-term coaching, but long-term support then needs another payer, usually a DD waiver, now closed to new people.

**Evidence.**
- VR closed Categories II and III on 9 December 2024 and estimates that in federal FY2027, 4,998 of 14,449 eligible people in Category II and 205 of 580 in Category III will not be served [31][32]. We found no reopening notice after February 2026 (not verified) [32].
- After VR closes a supported employment case, "the CRP or other identified provider is responsible for securing long-term funding" [31]. DD waiver employment services need a record that VR is not available or used up [7].
- The VR plan cites "Insufficient appropriation authority" as the reason the categories stay closed (not re-checked by a fact check) [31].
- School services end with a regular diploma or at 21; a certificate of attendance does not end them [33][34].
- Students 14 to 21 with an IEP or 504 plan can get Pre-Employment Transition Services, and keep them if later placed in a closed category; families who apply after graduation lose this protection [31].
- Nationally, 39.1% of young adults with autism got no services after high school [77].

**Cause.** State appropriation authority for the VR match; the waiver wait list; the VR-first rule.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly (FY2028 HB 2 and HB 10); DESE and VR; DMH with VR; school districts.

### Finding 3. The age 21 health care cliff

**What happens.** HCY covers medically necessary services under 21. At 21 it ends, and families often learn this when therapy, nursing, or supplies stop.

**Evidence (current manuals, confirmed by fact check).**
- Outpatient physical, occupational, and speech therapy are "not covered for MO HealthNet participants ages 21 and over," with narrow exceptions [36]. Expansion-group adults get 20 habilitative visits a year [42].
- Adult private duty nursing comes only through the Medically Complex Adult Waiver, which needs ICF/IID level of care and HCY nursing, or a documented need, before 21 [39].
- Chest wall oscillation vests and cough devices are covered only to age 20, and enteral formula only under 21; adults must use the exceptions process, which needs covered items "tried and found to be inappropriate or ineffective" [37][38].
- Adults get binaural hearing aids only "in cases of blindness," and eye exams every 24 months [40].
- A DD waiver can fund adult therapy, but enrollment is wait-listed [1][7].

**Cause.** MO HealthNet adult coverage rules; EPSDT applies only under 21.

**Who can fix it.** MO HealthNet Division; the General Assembly; DMH; DHSS; health systems.

### Finding 4. County differences in local DD funds

**What happens.** Families with the same needs get different help in different counties.

**Evidence.**
- SB40 boards need a voter-approved levy of up to four mills (40 cents per $100) [23]. The FY2027 budget counts on $5,889,538 of board tax money as Medicaid match [13].
- A 2020 association map shows no board in Barton, Cedar, Dade, Dallas, Henry, Hickory, McDonald, Ozark, St. Clair, and Vernon [22], but the DMH map shows all 10 as PfH counties, and the rule allows PfH where a board funds it or CMS approved the county [3][10]. The current board list is not verified.
- PfH is not available in Douglas and Wright [10]. Statewide PfH enrollment fell from 2,077 (FY2022) to 1,053 (FY2025) [8].
- Board help varies; for example, Stone County offers individual funding up to $1,000 a year [68].
- Without MO HealthNet, a DD-eligible person in Greene, Henry, Laclede, Ozark, Stone, or Taney gets only the statewide Information Specialist line; elsewhere a local County Support Specialist helps [11].
- No statute gives a right to appeal a board denial [23].
- HCS HB 3467 (2026), a sales tax option of up to 0.5% for DD services, passed two House committees 16 to 0 and 11 to 0 but did not reach the floor [18]. County boards list "Safeguard Property Taxes" as their first priority [22].

**Cause.** Optional, voter-dependent local funding (RSMo 205.968 to 205.971); PfH participation rules; DDD practice tying local coordination to MO HealthNet.

**Who can fix it.** County commissions and voters; SB40 boards; the General Assembly; DMH.

### Finding 5. The direct support workforce

**What happens.** A family with a funded plan may still wait for a worker.

**Evidence.**
- Missouri, 2024: DSP turnover 46.5% (median 37.5%; reporting states 37.1%); 43.9% of leavers had worked under six months; 28.0% of agencies turned away or stopped taking referrals; average wage $18.07 against the report's $20.87 living wage [29].
- DMH residential rates fund "a $17.02/hour wage for direct support professionals" [8].
- FY2027 law holds DD rates at the 1 January 2026 level and DHSS home care rates at the 1 January 2025 level; the self-directed personal assistant rate is $33.00 an hour ($36.76 medical exception) [13].
- Since 1 August 2026, no new CDS, personal care, or DME providers can enroll, for six months with possible extension to one year [54]. This may limit choice in rural counties (our inference).
- No regional workforce data are public [29].

**Cause.** Budget rate language (Sections 10.2020 and 10.2030); the enrollment moratorium.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly; DMH and DHSS; MO HealthNet with CMS; providers.

### Finding 6. Respite for adults under 63

**What happens.** Caregivers of adults with IDD who are not on a waiver have few ways to get a paid break.

**Evidence.**
- DHSS respite is only in the Aged and Disabled Waiver, age 63 and older [51].
- The Area Agency on Aging program serves "Older relatives, including parents, aged 55 or older providing care to adults with disabilities aged 18-59" [55].
- PfH has no respite [6]; other waiver respite is wait-listed [1].
- Missouri has no state respite coalition or Lifespan Respite program on the national map; whether it applied for the 2026 federal grant round (closed 22 July 2026) is not verified [56].
- Missouri spends 0.8% of IDD funds on family support (U.S. 10.6%) and reaches about 6% of caregiving families (U.S. 11%) [28].

**Cause.** Program age limits; state spending choices; no lifespan respite program.

**Who can fix it.** DHSS or DMH; the General Assembly; SB40 boards; Area Agencies on Aging.

### Finding 7. Housing and rural transport

**What happens.** Outside Springfield, adults often have no affordable accessible home and no ride to work, day programs, or care.

**Evidence.**
- HUD's 2023 table shows 23 Mainstream vouchers at the Springfield Housing Authority, 100 at the Jasper County agency, and none at the agency serving 10 counties around Springfield [57].
- The DMH Rental Assistance Program is closed "through January 2027" and serves only DMH mental health and substance use clients [58]. DD residential care is wait-listed [1].
- Access Express costs $2.00 a ride and runs only in fixed-route hours and the City Utilities area [59]. Rural OATS service runs on set days; Vernon County service to Nevada runs on the first Friday of the month [60].
- Medicaid rides need 2 business days notice in Greene and 3 elsewhere, do not go to waiver services, and carry an attendant only for riders under 21 [61].

**Cause.** Limited federal housing supply; local transit budgets; NEMT rules.

**Who can fix it.** Housing agencies; City Council and City Utilities; county commissions and OATS; MO HealthNet; DMH.

### Finding 8. No benefits counseling (WIPA) project for the region

**What happens.** Without expert advice, workers fear losing SSI, SSDI, or MO HealthNet, and some refuse work or lose benefits by mistake.

**Evidence.**
- The only Missouri WIPA project found covers St. Louis City and 47 counties, none in this region [35]. Which project serves the region is not verified; the Ticket to Work Help Line (1-866-968-7842) routes by ZIP code [35].
- Rules are complex: in 2026, substantial gainful activity is $1,690 a month (non-blind), a trial work month is over $1,210, and the Missouri 1619(b) threshold is $55,181 a year [86].
- Missouri's Medicaid buy-in for workers lapsed on 28 August 2025 and returned on 28 August 2026 [19].

**Cause.** SSA award areas; no state or local funding.

**Who can fix it.** SSA; VR and DMH; SB40 boards and providers; the General Assembly.

### Finding 9. Gaps in clinical evidence and adult testing

**What happens.** Adults need current clinical evidence for DD services, SSI, VR, and equipment, but many leave school with old scores and find no adult evaluator.

**Evidence.**
- Adults need limits in 2 or more areas for DD eligibility and 3 or more for a waiver [3][4][5].
- DDD accepts an adaptive test from the last 3 years (practice, not rule) [12].
- MO HealthNet pays for psychological testing up to "four (4) hours per participant, per performing provider, per rolling year" [41].
- The only autism diagnostic clinic found in Springfield serves "Ages 1 through 8" [43]; we found no adult service (search not exhaustive).
- Equipment claims are denied if the ordering clinician is not enrolled with MO HealthNet [37].

**Cause.** Testing limits; few adult evaluators; no duty to test at school exit.

**Who can fix it.** MO HealthNet; DMH; school districts; SB40 boards; health systems.

### Finding 10. Guardianship and paid family care

**What happens.** Parents are often told to seek guardianship at 18. A parent who becomes guardian cannot be the paid CDS attendant, so the family can lose income while the adult loses rights.

**Evidence.**
- "The attendant may be a family member. However, the attendant cannot be the participant's spouse or legal guardian" [50].
- School rights transfer at 18, with notice a year before [33].
- No Missouri statute defines a supported decision-making agreement or requires third parties to honor one; courts must consider less restrictive alternatives [85].
- Greene County's fee page lists a "$760.00 Advance Deposit," but not clearly for which case type; the cost is not verified [87].

**Cause.** The DHSS CDS rule; RSMo Chapter 475.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly; DHSS; probate courts; schools and support coordinators.

### Finding 12. Short appeal windows

**What happens.** A mailed notice's window to appeal and keep services can close before an overloaded caregiver reads it.

**Evidence.**
- MO HealthNet benefits continue only if the appeal is filed within 10 days "from the date of the mailing of notice" [48]; a hearing request is due within 90 days of the Action Notice [49].
- DHSS home care: the manual says 10 calendar days, a form says 10 business days (not verified which applies) [53].
- A family that loses may have to repay services received during the appeal [3][48][53].
- For DD waiver decisions, starting a DSS hearing ends the DMH appeal route [3].

**Cause.** RSMo 208.080.3; agency rules; notice design. **Who can fix it.** The General Assembly; DSS, DHSS, and DMH.

## 6. What works: navigation and application help

**Table 5. Evidence on navigation**

| Model | Study | Effect | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outreach letter only | Randomized trial, about 30,000 older adults likely eligible for SNAP [71] | Enrolled in 9 months: 6% (no contact) vs 11% | High |
| Outreach plus hands-on help | Same trial [71] | 18%, 3 times the no-contact rate | High |
| Family navigation by bilingual community health workers | Randomized trial, 249 families of toddlers with a positive autism screen [72] | Follow-through 85.7% vs 76.4%, hazard ratio 1.39 (95% CI 1.05–1.84); Hispanic families (two sites) 90.9% vs 53.3%, hazard ratio 2.81; mean 19.6 contacts, mostly calls and texts | High; child outcome, not adult services |
| Patient navigation (mostly cancer screening) | Meta-analysis of 13 trials [73] | Odds ratio 2.47 (95% CI 1.91–3.20) | Medium (summary only) |
| Community health workers | Randomized trial, 302 patients [74] | $2.47 returned to Medicaid per $1 within one fiscal year | Medium (abstract and press release) |
| Parent advocacy training | Pilot trial, 41 parents [75]; larger trial baseline [76] | More knowledge and advocacy skill; advocacy linked to more adult services (beta 0.33) | High for knowledge; low for service outcomes |
| "No Wrong Door" options counseling | Process evaluations only | No rigorous outcome trial found | Low |

Lessons: give hands-on help, not only information [71]; reach out to likely eligible groups (IEP students in their last two years, SSI youth near 18, the "case management only" caseload, caregivers 60+) and aim at the highest burden [72]; use local, matched navigators by text and phone with some visits [72]; train parents [75][76]. SB40 boards that provide DD support coordination can bill MO HealthNet Targeted Case Management for DD-eligible members [8]; help before eligibility has no Medicaid route we found. Under the wait list, success means "on the right list, at the right priority, using state plan services while waiting."

## 9. Methods and limits

**Methods.** An AI research team of eight expert roles (advocate, social scientist, physician, legal analyst, political action analyst, benefits planner, transition specialist, community living specialist) collected facts from public sources on 26–27 October 2026, preferring statutes, rules, budget bills, and agency manuals. Three fact checks reviewed the high-risk facts, found nothing invented, and corrected 21 facts; this report uses the corrected versions. Other facts rely on the cited sources.

**Facts that remain unverified:** whether emergency (PON 5) cases can enroll during the wait list; wait list counts; the $80.7 million add-back (news only; reported cut amounts and a proposed hourly rate are not used); which counties have SB40 boards; whether VR reopened Category II or III after February 2026; which WIPA project serves the region; the DHSS 10 calendar or business day window; the start and Missouri design of 2027 Medicaid work rules; Greene County guardianship costs; 2026 election results and 2027 committee posts; the 2024 to 2025 wait list history; whether Missouri applied for the 2026 Lifespan Respite grant.

**Limits.** Census counts exclude institutional residents, and ages 16 to 17 are estimated. County DD, wait list, and regional workforce data are not public. Several Section 6 studies concern children or other programs and apply here by analogy. Rules change; check sources before acting. No person or agency was contacted.

This report is policy analysis, not legal, medical, or financial advice. A family with a live deadline should contact Missouri Protection and Advocacy Services, legal aid, or a qualified professional.
