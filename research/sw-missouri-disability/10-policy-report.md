# Gaps in Disability Services in Southwest Missouri

**A gap and policy report on services for disabled young adults and adults (age 16 and older) and their caregivers**

Date: 27 October 2026

Prepared for: state lawmakers and state agencies; local governments and county SB40 developmental disability boards; providers and nonprofits.

## 1. Purpose

This report shows where the public service system fails disabled people age 16 and older, and their caregivers, in Southwest Missouri. It covers people with intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD), physical and medically complex conditions, and sensory disabilities. For each gap, it gives the effect on families, the evidence, the cause (a law, a rule, a budget line, or a practice), and the body that can fix it. It then gives numbered recommendations with dates. All facts come from public sources checked on 26–27 October 2026. Where our fact checks could not confirm a fact, the text says "not verified."

The region is the 23 counties served by the Department of Mental Health (DMH) Division of Developmental Disabilities (DDD) [9]. Springfield Regional Office: Christian, Dallas, Douglas, Greene, Hickory, Laclede, Ozark, Polk, Stone, Taney, Webster, Wright. Joplin Satellite Office: Barry, Barton, Cedar, Dade, Henry, Jasper, Lawrence, McDonald, Newton, St. Clair, Vernon.

**Terms used in this report.** DD: developmental disability. MO HealthNet: Missouri Medicaid. DSS: Department of Social Services; FSD: its Family Support Division. DHSS: Department of Health and Senior Services. DESE: Department of Elementary and Secondary Education. VR: Missouri Vocational Rehabilitation (part of DESE). Comprehensive, Community Support, and Partnership for Hope (PfH): the three adult DD Medicaid waivers. PON: priority of need, the score that orders the waiver wait list. ISP: individual support plan. CDS: consumer directed services, a DHSS home care model in which the person hires the attendant. DSP: direct support professional. HCY: Missouri's EPSDT benefit for MO HealthNet participants under 21. SB40 board: a county developmental disability board funded by a local property tax. WIPA: Work Incentives Planning and Assistance, the SSA benefits counseling program.

## 2. Summary

### 2.1 The problem

A disabled adult in Southwest Missouri must pass through many separate programs, and each program has its own gate, documents, office, and deadline. On 15 October 2026, the main gate for adults with developmental disabilities, a Medicaid waiver slot, closed to new enrollment with no end date, and the routes around it (job support, respite, home care, housing, and transport) are also limited. The families who must manage this are often rural, poor, or led by an aging caregiver, so they have the least time and help to get through it.

### 2.2 Main findings

1. **The DD waiver wait list has no end date, no named crisis exception, and no public count.** DMH put new enrollment in the Comprehensive, Community Support, and Partnership for Hope waivers on a wait list on 15 October 2026 [1]. The FY2027 budget law limits new slots to 127 named exceptions [13]. DMH publishes no wait list numbers [2].
2. **Two wait lists meet at school exit.** VR has served only Priority Category I since 9 December 2024 [31][32]. A school leaver with IDD may get a job through VR but have no payer for long-term job support, because that payer is usually a DD waiver [7][31].
3. **At age 21, MO HealthNet coverage narrows sharply.** Outpatient therapies, private duty nursing, and several devices stop or move to a narrow exception process [36][37][38][39].
4. **The direct support workforce cannot deliver all the services that exist.** In 2024, Missouri DSP turnover was 46.5%, and 28.0% of provider agencies turned away referrals for lack of staff [29]. The FY2027 budget law holds DD and home care rates flat [13].
5. **Help depends on the county.** County SB40 funds, Partnership for Hope, and local help for people without Medicaid differ by county [10][11][22].
6. **Respite and benefits counseling have large holes.** Most adults with IDD under 63 who are not on a waiver have no paid respite route [51][55][56]. We found no WIPA benefits counseling project for the region [35].
7. **Families carry the paperwork, and official information is sometimes wrong.** We found errors in office addresses, phone numbers, deadlines, and benefit charts [12][47][64][65][66][67]. The best-tested fix is hands-on application help: in one large trial, it raised enrollment from 6% to 18% [71].

### 2.3 Top recommendation for each audience

| Audience | Top recommendation | When |
|---|---|---|
| State legislature and budget | Fund enrollment for people on the DD waiver wait list in a FY2027 supplemental and in the FY2028 House Bill 10, with Part 2 slot language that matches the money. Require DMH to post the monthly unmet need report. | Supplemental: January to March 2027. FY2028 budget: before 6 p.m. Friday 7 May 2027 |

- **Disability is more common in older, rural counties.** In Hickory, Ozark, St. Clair, Barton, Dallas, and Vernon counties, 24% to 29% of adults report a disability, against 17.7% statewide [26].
- **Poverty is high.** In Ozark, Vernon, Barton, Wright, Hickory, and St. Clair counties, 30% to 37% of disabled adults live below the poverty line [26].

- **Families do most of the care.** Statewide, about 73% of people with IDD live with a family caregiver, and about 1 in 4 of those caregivers is 60 or older (modeled estimates, FY2023) [28].

## 4. How families move through the system

### 4.1 The chain of prerequisites

Most adult services have a gate, and most gates need another program first. A family must clear the gates in order. A delay at one gate delays every gate after it.

**Table 2. Gates and what must come first**

| Gate | What must come first | Source |
|---|---|---|
| DMH DD eligibility | Records that show the disability began before age 22. An adaptive test (the Missouri Adaptive Ability Scale, or a valid test from the last 3 years). Adults need substantial limits in 2 or more major life areas. | [3][4][12] |
| DD support coordination and new DD funding | DD eligibility. Active MO HealthNet for new funding requests (DDD practice). In Greene, Henry, Laclede, Ozark, Stone, and Taney counties, a DD-eligible person without MO HealthNet gets only a statewide phone line, not a local coordinator. | [11] |
| DD waiver slot | DD eligibility; MO HealthNet; ICF/IID level of care with limits in 3 or more areas; an ISP, budget, and PON through Utilization Review. Since 15 October 2026, a place on the wait list. | [1][3][5][7] |
| MO HealthNet on the basis of disability | A disability decision by SSA or the state. A separate FSD application, because SSI does not bring MO HealthNet in Missouri (a "209(b)" state). | [45] |
| DHSS home care (personal care, CDS) | MO HealthNet active on the referral date. An assessment score of 18 or more. | [52] |
| VR job placement | A VR application; an open priority category (only Category I is open); a VR plan before school exit. | [31][32] |
| Long-term job support after VR | A long-term funder, usually a DD waiver (now wait-listed). | [7][31] |

### 4.3 Why this creates administrative burden

Researchers divide the cost of dealing with a public program into three parts [69]:

- **Learning costs:** finding out that a program exists, who qualifies, and how to apply. DD eligibility, MO HealthNet, SSI, VR, DHSS home care, and county boards each have a separate front door. Many families do not know that an SSI award does not bring MO HealthNet [45].
- **Compliance costs:** forms, records, assessments, renewals, and deadlines. Families must prove onset before 22, keep MO HealthNet active, and meet appeal windows as short as 10 days from the date a notice is mailed [48]. A 50-state study found Medicaid application forms written at grade 11 to 18 [80]. Nationally, 69% of people who lost Medicaid during the 2023–2024 "unwinding" lost it for procedural reasons [79].
- **Psychological costs:** stress, stigma, loss of control, and fear of losing benefits by working or saving.

Burden falls hardest on people with the fewest resources. Stress and scarcity reduce the same mental resources (such as planning and memory) that complex processes demand [70]. An exhausted or aging caregiver, and an adult with a cognitive disability, are both caught in this trap. Distance adds cost: when SSA field offices closed, disability applications in nearby areas fell by about 10%, and the largest drops were among people with low education and low earnings [78].

A fourth problem looks the same to a family but has a different cause: **supply rationing.** Wait lists, closed VR categories, and agencies without staff ration services even when a family does every step right. Navigation help can reduce burden. It cannot create a slot or a worker. Programs should measure the two problems separately.

## 5. Findings: the gaps

Each finding gives what happens to families, the evidence, the cause, and who can fix it.

### Finding 1. The DD waiver wait list has no end date, no named crisis exception, and no public count

**What happens to families.** A person can be found DD eligible and still get no waiver services. Since 15 October 2026, new enrollment in the Comprehensive, Community Support, and Partnership for Hope waivers goes to a wait list "due to current budget limitations," with "no estimated end date" [1]. Current participants keep services. New people keep access only if they are paid through Inter-Divisional Agreements or their participation is "mandated by federal rule." The children's MOCDD waiver continues as usual [1].

**Evidence.**
- The memo names no crisis or emergency exception [1]. The DMH rule gives emergency cases (PON 5) the highest priority on the list, which is a place in line, not a slot [3]. Whether an emergency case can enroll now is not verified.
- HB 2010, Section 10.2015: "No funds shall be expended in furtherance of waiver slots assigned on July 1, 2026 except for" 50 crisis residential, 27 nursing home transition, 39 Children's Division transition, and 11 MOCDD transition slots, 127 in all [13].
- Section 10.3005 already requires DMH to send the House Budget and Senate Appropriations chairs and ranking members a monthly count of "individuals presenting for service that have not been assigned a waiver slot," with the cost to serve them [13]. The DMH wait list report section shows no numbers [2].
- No rule sets a time limit to move from the list to a slot. The emergency criteria count loss of a caregiver when it happens, not when it is predictable, so the list rewards late contact [3].
- Even before the wait list, 41.0% of the statewide DD caseload (17,912 people, FY2025) had support coordination or information support only. In the region, the share was 30.4% [8].
- This is the second recent freeze. News reports say a wait list began 1 July 2024 and was cleared after a supplemental budget signed 17 March 2025 (history from news only) [15][30].
- For FY2027, the legislature reversed the Governor's proposed cuts to DD self-directed supports and day habilitation. News reports put the total at about $80.7 million (all funds); no primary budget document gives this figure [16][17]. The state projects a FY2028 shortfall of more than $500 million [14].

**Cause.** Budget law (HB 2010 Sections 10.2015 and 10.410) and DMH practice under 9 CSR 45-2.015 and 2.017.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly (House Budget Committee and its Health, Mental Health and Social Services appropriations subcommittee; Senate Appropriations), through a FY2027 supplemental and the FY2028 HB 10. The Governor, through the budget recommendation. DMH, through public reporting and rule time standards.

### Finding 2. At school exit, the VR closure meets the waiver wait list

**What happens to families.** A student with IDD who leaves school in 2026–2027 can face two wait lists at once. If VR places the student in Category II or III, the student waits for VR. If VR serves the student (Category I), VR can fund job placement and short-term job coaching. Then a long-term funder must take over, and for most adults with IDD that funder is a DD waiver, which is now closed to new people.

**Evidence.**
- VR closed Priority Categories II and III on 9 December 2024 [31][32]. VR estimates that in federal fiscal year 2027, 4,998 of 14,449 eligible people in Category II and 205 of 580 in Category III will not be served [31]. We found no notice of reopening after February 2026 (not verified) [32].
- After VR closes a supported employment case, "the CRP or other identified provider is responsible for securing long-term funding" [31]. DD waiver employment services need a record that VR is not available, not applicable, or used up [7].
- The VR plan states that "Insufficient appropriation authority" keeps the categories closed (quoted in our transition research; not re-checked by a fact check) [31].
- School services end with a regular diploma or at age 21. A certificate of attendance does not end them [33][34].
- Students 14 to 21 with an IEP or 504 plan can get Pre-Employment Transition Services (Pre-ETS) without a VR application. A student who starts Pre-ETS before VR places them in a closed category keeps Pre-ETS [31]. A family that waits until after graduation loses this protection.
- National studies show services drop after high school: 39.1% of young adults with autism got no services after high school [77].

**Cause.** State appropriation authority for the VR match; the waiver wait list; the VR-first rule for waiver employment services.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly (FY2028 HB 2 for DESE and VR, and HB 10 for DMH); DESE and VR; DMH and VR together (a written agreement on what counts as VR "not available"); school districts.

### Finding 3. The age 21 health care cliff

**What happens to families.** Under 21, HCY covers medically necessary services. At 21 this ends, and families often learn of it only when therapy, nursing, or supplies stop.

**Evidence (current MO HealthNet manuals, confirmed by our fact check).**
- Outpatient physical, occupational, and speech therapy are "not covered for MO HealthNet participants ages 21 and over," with narrow exceptions [36]. Adults in the expansion group get 20 habilitative therapy visits a year [42].
- Private duty nursing for adults is available only through the Medically Complex Adult Waiver. Entry needs ICF/IID level of care and private duty nursing under HCY, or a documented need, before 21 [39].
- Chest wall oscillation vests and cough devices are covered only to age 20, and enteral formula only under 21. Adults must use the exceptions process, which needs proof that covered items were "tried and found to be inappropriate or ineffective" [37][38].
- Adults get binaural hearing aids only "in cases of blindness," and eye exams every 24 months instead of 12 [40].
- A DD waiver can pay for therapy after 21, but new enrollment is wait-listed [1][7].

**Cause.** MO HealthNet adult coverage rules; federal EPSDT applies only under 21.

**Who can fix it.** MO HealthNet Division (coverage policy); the General Assembly (funding); DMH (waiver access); DHSS (an adult nursing path for people who do not meet ICF/IID level of care); health systems (plan the change at 19).

### Finding 4. County differences in local DD funds

**What happens to families.** Two families with the same needs can get different help because they live in different counties.

**Evidence.**
- SB40 boards fund services that Medicaid does not cover. A board needs a voter-approved levy of up to four mills per dollar of assessed value (40 cents per $100) [23]. The FY2027 budget counts on $5,889,538 of county board tax money as Medicaid match [13].
- A 2020 county boards association map shows no board in Barton, Cedar, Dade, Dallas, Henry, Hickory, McDonald, Ozark, St. Clair, and Vernon [22]. But the DMH map shows all 10 as PfH counties, and the rule lets PfH run where a board funds it or where CMS approved the county [3][10]. The current board list is not verified.
- PfH is not available in Douglas and Wright counties [10]. Statewide PfH enrollment fell from 2,077 (FY2022) to 1,053 (FY2025) [8].
- Board help varies. For example, the Stone County board offers individual funding of up to $1,000 a year [68].
- A DD-eligible person without MO HealthNet in Greene, Henry, Laclede, Ozark, Stone, or Taney County gets only the statewide DDD Information Specialist line. In the other 17 counties, a local County Support Specialist helps [11].
- No statute gives a person denied board help a right to appeal [23].
- HCS HB 3467 (2026) would let a county ask voters for a sales tax of up to 0.5% for DD services if SB40 property tax revenue falls, or where no board exists. It passed two House committees 16 to 0 and 11 to 0 but did not reach the floor [18]. County boards named "Safeguard Property Taxes" as their first 2026 priority [22].

**Cause.** State law makes local DD funding optional and dependent on voters (RSMo 205.968 to 205.971). PfH depends on county participation or CMS approval. DDD practice ties local coordination to MO HealthNet.

**Who can fix it.** County commissions and voters; SB40 boards; the General Assembly (a sales tax option; protection of levies in property tax changes); DMH.

### Finding 5. The direct support workforce

**What happens to families.** A family with a funded plan may still wait for a worker. Authorized hours are not delivered hours.

**Evidence.**
- In 2024, Missouri DSP turnover was 46.5% (mean; median 37.5%), against a weighted mean of 37.1% across reporting states. Of DSPs who left, 43.9% had worked less than six months. 28.0% of agencies turned away or stopped taking referrals because of staffing. The average wage was $18.07 an hour, below the $20.87 "living wage" figure in the report [29].
- DMH residential rates were funded to support "a $17.02/hour wage for direct support professionals" [8].
- The FY2027 budget law holds DD provider rates at the 1 January 2026 level and DHSS home care rates at the 1 January 2025 level. The self-directed personal assistant rate is $33.00 an hour ($36.76 with a medical exception) [13].
- Since 1 August 2026, MO HealthNet has not enrolled new CDS, personal care, or DME providers. The moratorium runs six months and may extend to one year; enrolled providers are not affected [54]. In rural counties with few vendors, this may limit choice (our inference; not measured).
- No regional workforce data are public [29].

**Cause.** Budget rate language (HB 2010 Sections 10.2020 and 10.2030); the provider enrollment moratorium.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly (FY2028 rates); DMH and DHSS (rate studies and wage reporting); MO HealthNet with CMS (moratorium terms); providers (data).

### Finding 6. Respite for adults under 63

**What happens to families.** Caregivers of adults with IDD who are not on a DD waiver have few ways to get a paid break.

**Evidence.**
- DHSS respite is available only in the Aged and Disabled Waiver, for participants 63 or older [51].
- The Area Agency on Aging caregiver program serves "Older relatives, including parents, aged 55 or older providing care to adults with disabilities aged 18-59" [55]. A younger parent does not qualify.
- PfH does not include respite [6]. Comprehensive and Community Support waiver respite is now wait-listed [1].
- The national respite network lists no Missouri state respite coalition or Lifespan Respite program [56]. A federal Lifespan Respite grant round closed on 22 July 2026; whether Missouri applied is not verified [56].
- Missouri spends 0.8% of IDD funds on family support, against 10.6% nationally, and reaches about 6% of IDD caregiving families, against 11% nationally (FY2023) [28].

**Cause.** Age limits in DHSS and federal caregiver programs; state spending choices; no state lifespan respite program.

**Who can fix it.** DHSS or DMH (grant application and registry); the General Assembly (match and family support funds); SB40 boards; Area Agencies on Aging.

### Finding 7. Housing and rural transport

**What happens to families.** Adults who need an affordable, accessible home, or a ride to work, day programs, or appointments, often have no option outside Springfield.

**Evidence.**
- HUD's 2023 table shows 23 Mainstream vouchers (for non-elderly people with disabilities) at the Springfield Housing Authority, 100 at the Jasper County Public Housing Agency, and none at the agency that serves 10 counties around Springfield [57].
- The DMH Rental Assistance Program is closed "through January 2027" and serves only people in DMH mental health or substance use services [58]. DD residential services come through the Comprehensive Waiver, which is wait-listed [1].
- Access Express paratransit in Springfield costs $2.00 a ride and runs only during fixed-route hours in the City Utilities service area [59].
- Rural OATS service runs on set days. For example, Vernon County service to Nevada runs on the first Friday of the month [60].
- Medicaid rides need 2 business days notice in Greene County and 3 in the other counties. They do not go to DD or DHSS waiver services, and they carry a parent or attendant only for riders under 21 [61].

**Cause.** Limited federal housing supply; local transit budgets; NEMT rules.

**Who can fix it.** Public housing agencies; City Council and City Utilities; county commissions and OATS; MO HealthNet (NEMT rules); DMH (housing funds).

### Finding 8. No benefits counseling (WIPA) project for the region

**What happens to families.** Workers fear that a job will cost them SSI, SSDI, or MO HealthNet. Without expert advice, some refuse work or lose benefits by mistake.

**Evidence.**
- The only Missouri WIPA project we found lists St. Louis City and 47 counties; none of the 23 counties in this region is on its list [35]. Which project serves the region is not verified. The SSA Ticket to Work Help Line (1-866-968-7842) routes callers by ZIP code [35].
- The work rules are complex. In 2026, SSDI substantial gainful activity is $1,690 a month (non-blind), a trial work month is any month over $1,210, and the Missouri 1619(b) threshold to keep Medicaid is $55,181 a year [86].
- Missouri's Medicaid buy-in for workers lapsed on 28 August 2025 and returned on 28 August 2026 under HB 2372 [19].

**Cause.** SSA award areas; no state or local funding for benefits counseling in the region.

**Who can fix it.** SSA; VR and DMH (contracted benefits planning); SB40 boards and providers; the General Assembly (keep the buy-in stable).

### Finding 9. Gaps in clinical evidence and adult testing

**What happens to families.** To qualify for DD services, SSI, VR, or equipment, an adult needs current, specific clinical evidence. Many young adults leave school with old test scores and cannot find an adult evaluator.

**Evidence.**
- Adults need substantial limits in 2 or more major life areas for DD eligibility, and 3 or more for a DD waiver [3][4][5]. A person can be DD eligible and still not reach a waiver.
- DDD accepts an adaptive test from the last 3 years (DDD practice, not rule) [12].
- MO HealthNet pays for psychological testing up to "four (4) hours per participant, per performing provider, per rolling year" [41].
- The only autism diagnostic clinic our team found in Springfield lists assessments for "Ages 1 through 8" [43]. We found no adult autism diagnostic service in Springfield (our search was not exhaustive).
- MO HealthNet denies an equipment claim if the ordering clinician is not enrolled with MO HealthNet [37].

**Cause.** MO HealthNet testing limits; few adult evaluators; no federal duty to test a student before school exit.

**Who can fix it.** MO HealthNet; DMH (written guidance on accepted tests); school districts (exit reevaluations on request); SB40 boards (fund adult testing); health systems.

### Finding 10. Guardianship and paid family care

**What happens to families.** Parents are often told to seek guardianship at 18. A parent who becomes legal guardian cannot be paid as the adult's CDS attendant. The family can lose income, and the adult can lose rights, at the same time.

**Evidence.**
- DHSS rule: "The attendant may be a family member. However, the attendant cannot be the participant's spouse or legal guardian" [50].
- School rights transfer to the student at 18, with notice one year before [33].
- Missouri has no statute that defines a supported decision-making agreement or requires schools, hospitals, or banks to honor one [85]. The guardianship statute requires courts to consider less restrictive alternatives [85].
- Families pay the court costs. The Greene County fee page lists a "$760.00 Advance Deposit," but its layout does not show which case type it applies to; the cost is not verified [87].

**Cause.** The DHSS CDS rule; RSMo Chapter 475; no supported decision-making statute.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly; DHSS; probate courts; schools and support coordinators.

### Finding 12. Short appeal windows

**What happens to families.** A notice arrives by mail, and the window to appeal and keep services can close before an overloaded caregiver reads it.

**Evidence.**
- MO HealthNet: to keep benefits, a person must appeal within 10 days "from the date of the mailing of notice" [48]. The hearing request itself is due within 90 days of the Action Notice [49].
- DHSS home care: the manual says 10 calendar days from the mail date; a state form says 10 business days. Which is right is not verified [53].
- A family that loses an appeal may have to repay the cost of services received during it [3][48][53].
- For DD waiver decisions, starting a DSS fair hearing ends the DMH appeal route [3].

**Cause.** RSMo 208.080.3; agency rules; notice design.

**Who can fix it.** The General Assembly; DSS and DHSS (align windows and write plain notices); DMH (amend 9 CSR 45-2.017(10)(A) or explain the trade-off).

## 6. What works: navigation and application help

**Table 5. Evidence on navigation**

| Model | Study | Effect | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outreach letter only | Randomized trial, about 30,000 older adults likely eligible for SNAP [71] | Enrolled in 9 months: 6% (no contact) vs 11% (letter) | High |
| Outreach plus hands-on application help | Same trial [71] | 18% enrolled, 3 times the no-contact rate | High |
| Family navigation by bilingual community health workers | Randomized trial, 249 families of toddlers with a positive autism screen [72] | Diagnostic follow-through in 1 year: 85.7% vs 76.4%; hazard ratio 1.39 (95% CI 1.05–1.84). Hispanic families (two sites): 90.9% vs 53.3%; hazard ratio 2.81. Mean 19.6 contacts per family, mostly calls and texts | High, but the outcome is a child diagnosis, not adult services |
| Patient navigation (mostly cancer screening) | Meta-analysis of 13 trials [73] | Odds of completing screening 2.47 times usual care (95% CI 1.91–3.20) | Medium (summary only) |
| Community health workers for Medicaid patients | Randomized trial, 302 patients [74] | $2.47 returned to Medicaid for each $1 spent, within one fiscal year | Medium (abstract and press release) |
| Parent advocacy training for transition | Pilot trial, 41 parents [75]; baseline data from a larger trial [76] | More knowledge and advocacy skill; parent advocacy was linked to more adult services (beta 0.33) | High for knowledge; low for service outcomes |
| Aging and Disability Resource Centers, "No Wrong Door" counseling | Process evaluations only | No rigorous outcome trial found | Low |

What this means for Southwest Missouri:

1. **Give help, not only information.** Information alone roughly doubled enrollment from a low base. Information plus hands-on help tripled it [71].
2. **Go to the family, and aim at the highest burden.** Good outreach lists: students with an IEP in their last two school years, SSI youth near 18, people on the DD "case management only" caseload, and caregivers 60 or older. Navigation helped most where barriers were greatest [72].
3. **Use local, matched navigators, mostly by text and phone, with a few home visits** [72]. Add transport or video in rural counties.
4. **Train parents to advocate**, so one navigator reaches more families [75][76].
5. **Use existing funds.** SB40 boards that provide DD support coordination can bill MO HealthNet through DD Targeted Case Management for DD-eligible members [8]. Help before eligibility has no Medicaid billing route that we found.
6. **Do not promise what supply cannot deliver.** Under the wait list, success means "on the right list, at the right priority, and using state plan services while waiting."

## 9. Methods and limits

**Methods.** An AI research team of eight expert roles (disability advocate, social scientist, physician, legal analyst, political action analyst, benefits planner, transition and employment specialist, and community living and home care specialist) collected facts from public sources on 26–27 October 2026. The team preferred primary sources: statutes, rules, budget bills, agency manuals, and official program pages. Three fact checks then reviewed the high-risk facts (eligibility rules, deadlines, dollar amounts, contact details, budget text, and legislator status). They found no invented facts. They corrected 21 facts, which this report uses in corrected form. Facts the fact checks did not review rely on the sources cited.

**Main facts that remain unverified.**
- Whether any emergency (PON 5) case can enroll during the waiver wait list. The memo is silent.
- The number of people on the wait list. DMH has not published it.
- The $80.7 million FY2027 add-back for self-directed supports and day habilitation (news sources only). News figures for the Governor's proposed cuts and a proposed hourly rate could not be confirmed, so this report does not use them.
- Which of the 23 counties have an SB40 board today (the 2020 association map and the DMH PfH map disagree).
- Whether VR has reopened Category II or III since February 2026.
- Which WIPA project serves the region.
- The DHSS keep-services window (10 calendar days or 10 business days).
- The start date and Missouri design of the 2027 Medicaid work rules.
- Greene County guardianship costs.
- 2026 election results and 2027 committee assignments.
- The 2024 to 2025 wait list history (news sources only).
- Whether Missouri applied for the 2026 federal Lifespan Respite grant.

**Limits.** Census counts exclude people in institutions and some group homes, and the 16 to 17 age group is estimated. County DD counts, wait list counts, and regional workforce data are not public. Several research findings in Section 6 concern children or other programs, so they apply to adult disability services by analogy. Rules and budgets change; check each source before acting. No person or agency was contacted.

This report is for policy analysis. It is not legal, medical, or financial advice. A family with a live deadline should contact Missouri Protection and Advocacy Services, legal aid, or a qualified professional.
